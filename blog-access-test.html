The Access Test: Where Does Your Extra Money Actually Go?

Mortgage, kids' activities, 401(k), savings. Which one can you actually reach?

Wayne M. Carr 2 min read
← Back to All Posts
Cash Flow

Picture a family with $500 left at the end of the month. Soccer fees, a weekend trip, a credit card payment, an extra mortgage payment, and the 401(k) match all want it. Every one is a reasonable choice. The question that matters: if I need this money back, how do I get it, and what does it cost me?

Extra on the mortgage lowers what you owe, but using that equity usually means applying for a loan, and approval is not guaranteed. A 401(k) up to the match is real value, but it is exposed to market ups and downs, and taking money out early can mean taxes and penalties. A savings account is easy to reach, though growth is usually slow and the interest is generally taxable.

None of these is wrong. Your money has two jobs: what it does when you spend it, and what it can still do for you afterward. Most families only ever give it the first job.

The Access Test

Before you send your next extra dollar anywhere, ask these four questions. Five minutes, no finance degree required.

  1. Where does my extra money go each month, in order?
  2. If I needed $3,000 to $5,000 next month, which of those places could I get it from?
  3. How long would it take, and what would it cost me?
  4. Is any of my money working for me in more than one way?

Extra mortgage payments, 401(k) contributions, savings, and family spending can all be sound choices. The point is to see the whole picture, so a good use of money today does not become a problem when an opportunity or a surprise shows up. Rates, taxes, and approvals vary, so nothing here is guaranteed. Talk with a licensed professional about your own situation.
